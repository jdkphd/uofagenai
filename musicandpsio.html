Music × Physiology

What happens in your body when you listen to music?

Music does more than shape our mood. Researchers study how listening can interact with the heart, the brain, stress hormones, and the autonomic nervous system. The answers are often more complicated than "music is relaxing."

The Big Picture

Four systems music may influence

Stress and emotion involve linked systems throughout the body. Here is a simplified overview of the areas researchers measure most often.

🧠

The Brain

Listening engages auditory, emotional, and reward-related regions. Strong emotional responses to music have been linked to activity in areas involved in reward and emotion.

❤️

Heart & Breathing

Researchers track heart rate and respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), which is the natural variation in heart rate with each breath. RSA is used as a marker of parasympathetic ("rest and digest") activity.

⚗️

Stress Hormones

The hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis releases cortisol during stress. Cortisol can be measured conveniently in saliva.

⚡

Nervous System

The sympathetic ("fight or flight") branch can be tracked with salivary alpha-amylase (sAA), a newer saliva-based marker of sympathetic activity.

Study Spotlight

Does relaxing music protect us from stress?

Thoma and colleagues (2013, PLOS ONE) tested whether listening to relaxing music before a stressful task would reduce the body's stress response.

How it worked

  • 60 healthy female volunteers (average age about 25)
  • Each person was randomly assigned to one of three 10-minute conditions:
🎵 Relaxing music (Allegri's "Miserere") 💧 Sound of rippling water 🤫 Rest in silence
  • Everyone then completed the Trier Social Stress Test (a mock job interview plus mental arithmetic in front of an audience).
  • Researchers measured cortisol, alpha-amylase, heart rate, RSA, perceived stress, and anxiety.

What they found

  • Cortisol: After controlling for depression, anxiety, chronic stress, and emotion regulation, the music group had the highest cortisol response and the water-sound group had the lowest.
  • Recovery: Alpha-amylase returned to baseline faster in the music group (about 25 minutes after the test, compared with about 40 minutes for the other groups).
  • Heart rate & feelings: No significant differences between groups in heart rate, perceived stress, or anxiety.
  • Surprise: The water sound was linked to faster RSA recovery than silence.
Why it matters: Music did not simply "turn down" stress. Its effects appeared to depend on timing and on which body system was measured. The researchers suggested that the timing of music relative to stress (before, during, or after) may matter. Because the study only included young, healthy women, the results may not generalize to other groups.

Key Takeaways

What we can (and can't) conclude

  1. Music interacts with several linked body systems, not just mood.
  2. Different systems can respond differently. A faster nervous-system recovery can occur alongside a higher hormone response.
  3. Timing and context likely matter: listening before, during, or after stress may have different effects.
  4. Natural sounds, like flowing water, deserve more research as a comparison to music.
  5. Single studies have limits. Look for replication and diverse samples before drawing firm conclusions.
